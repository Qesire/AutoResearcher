# Constructive, evidence-calibrated scientific expression

This guidance applies across scientific reasoning, writing, reviewing and presentations. It refines sentence construction without weakening experimental integrity. Follow the user's overarching work-and-expression instructions.

## Compose through information and relationships

- Name the object, action and observation directly. Start a paragraph from the strongest useful result, mathematical dependence, measured effect or competing explanation; carry known information into a new relationship in the next sentence.
- Prefer precise nouns and verbs over vague nominalizations, decorative modifiers and generic claims of importance. Present the causal or mathematical relation rather than narrating what the author intends to do.
- Let claims carry their own scope: configuration, comparator, metric, source, time window or mathematical assumption belongs beside the conclusion it limits. This is more informative than a preliminary disclaimer.
- Use transitions to explain a mechanism, contrast, consequence or dependency. A paragraph should move the argument forward, not alternate mechanically between praise and caveats.

## Replace defensive and gratuitously negative patterns

Common low-information openings include "We do not claim ...", "It should be noted that ...", "This does not necessarily mean ...", long lists of possible confounders without distinguishing predictions, and repeated "not X but Y" where Y can be stated directly.

Prefer a statement with an explicit subject, observable and inferential status:

| Defensive or vague | Constructive direction |
| --- | --- |
| "This does not prove that the method always works." | "The current measurements cover [conditions]; [unmeasured condition] is the remaining generalization test." |
| "We do not intend to optimize the whole system." | "The intervention modifies [subsystem]; system-level speed is assessed separately." |
| "The result may possibly suggest some relationship." | "Across [data], [X] covaries with [Y]; [specific test] would distinguish the competing explanations." |
| "This approach is not entirely new." | "It shares [mechanism] with [prior work] and changes [specific operation or condition]." |

These are patterns for editing, not factual assertions. Replace the bracketed terms with verified facts or preserve the original uncertainty.

**Necessary negation remains essential.** State exact impossibility theorems, failed hypotheses, lower bounds, excluded populations, unavailable measurements and negative observations clearly. A negative result can close one action class while exposing a different scientific question. Never turn a real failure into a positive result through wording.

## Calibrate certainty through evidence

- **Derived:** "Under assumptions A and B, equation (n) implies ...".
- **Measured:** "In [setting], [metric] changed by [amount relative to comparator]."
- **Observed, mechanism open:** "The measurements are consistent with [mechanism]; [alternative] predicts [distinct effect]."
- **Proposed:** "We hypothesize [mechanism] and test it using [discriminator]."
- **Not measured:** "The influence on [target] remains to be measured."

Use *may*, *might*, *could*, *suggests* or *is consistent with* when the inference actually requires them, not as a universal prefix. Say "does not" when falsification or exclusion is demonstrated; state exactly what failed. A simulation, analytic equality, code inspection, or proxy does not automatically establish a physical or end-to-end result.

## Edit at sentence level

Choose the most meaningful positive clause as the grammatical center. Keep required contrast, limits or negation after the main proposition when it changes interpretation. Remove decorative em dashes and gratuitous compounds; keep meaningful hyphenation, ranges, mathematical minus signs and terminology. Tense follows the proposition: established knowledge and interpretations typically use present; completed experiments use past; planned work uses future/conditional. Purposeful changes in tense can occur within a paragraph.

**Review test:** After editing, can a reader identify the new scientific information, its evidence, its exact scope, and how it advances the preceding sentence? If an edit deletes a real result or makes a proposition stronger than its evidence, restore the original content.
